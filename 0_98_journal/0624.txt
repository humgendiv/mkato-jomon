Ancient genomes reveal demographic trajectories during the Classic Maya period

Highlights
•Ancient genomes recovered from seven individuals at Classic Copán
•Local ancestry persisted in the Maya region from the Late Archaic period
•Gene flow into Copán identified during the Early Classic period
•Genetic evidence of population decline coincided with the civilization’s collapse

Summary
Copán was a major capital at the southeasternmost extreme of the Classic Maya civilization, serving as a crossroads connecting Central and South America. In 426/427 CE, the city witnessed the establishment of a royal dynasty, which endured for approximately 400 years. Despite extensive historical and archaeological records, there remains limited information regarding the genetic profile of people who resided in Copán. Here, we present genomes from seven Classic Copán individuals, including a potential member of the ruling dynasty and an accompanying sacrificial burial. Our analysis shows that the population associated with the Classic Copán culture forms a genetic cluster with the Late Archaic population from Belize, as well as with the Terminal Classic Maya from Chichén Itzá, the Colonial Maya from Campeche, and present-day Maya, all from Mexico. Using Late Archaic Belize as a source of local ancestry, we identify an influx of ancestry associated with highland Mexican populations from the Early-to-Middle Classic period onward, underscoring the role of gene flow in the formation of the Classic Maya state. Estimates of effective population size suggest a decline at the end of the Classic period, when Classic Maya civilization experienced widespread destabilization and collapse.

Introduction
Copán is one of the most significant centers of the Classic Maya civilization (circa 250–950 CE). The earliest human occupation at Copán goes back at least to the Early Preclassic period (prior to circa 1,000 BCE). During this era, the Copán Valley, located in western Honduras near the border of Guatemala, saw the emergence of small farming communities, primarily relying on maize-based food production.1,2 Monumental construction, such as Maya architecture and inscriptions,3 began in this region during the Early Classic period (circa 300–400 CE). A royal dynasty was founded in 426/427 CE, according to the current results of epigraphic decipherment.4 The dynastic succession saw a series of 16 rulers over the ancient city and its polity. Copán thrived as a political, economic, and ceremonial hub, with both elite and commoner residential structures identified at the site. Although the population is estimated to have peaked at around 30,000 inhabitants,5 the political regime ultimately collapsed circa 820 CE at the end of the Late Classic period.3,6,7
A long-standing hypothesis on the formation of the Copán kingdom proposes the presence of an enclave of Maya culture within a territory inhabited by non-Maya people.3,8,9 It is suggested that individuals belonging to a widely dispersed Maya elite network migrated to the region, potentially from the central Petén region of Guatemala, especially around Tikal area, where they intermarried with local non-Maya residents. Archaeological evidence points to extensive trade and political connections between Copán and other Mesoamerican cities, such as Tikal, Kaminaljuyú in the Valley of Guatemala, and Teotihuacán in the Valley of Mexico.2,3,6 Inscriptions from Copán reveal that the first dynastic founder, K’inich Yax K’uk’ Mo’, was an outsider who assumed power in 426 CE and arrived at Copan in 427 CE.4,10,11 Isotopic analysis of tooth enamel provides further support for extensive mobility, uncovering significant variation in the places of origin among human remains discovered at Copán.12 Notably, this variation is more pronounced among elite burials, including the first and subsequent kings.13,14 As the chemistry of tooth enamel reflects the foods and liquids consumed during infancy and early childhood,15 this suggests that these elites grew up outside of Copán before relocating to the city, where they were ultimately buried. However, isotopic analysis cannot identify non-local individuals beyond first generation immigrants. Genetic data are required to explore long-term interactions between local and non-local populations for a better understanding of subsequent social and cultural change.
Previous analyses of ancient genomes in the Maya region16,17,18,19 have uncovered the presence of a distinct genetic ancestry dating back to the Early Holocene, approximately 9,300–7,300 years before present (BP),17 which constitutes a lineage likely diverged from the initial southward migrations through the Americas. By the Late Archaic period, around 5,600–3,700 years BP, the region saw gene flow from further south.16 Although a later influx of highland Mexican ancestry—represented by indigenous groups from Oaxaca, Mixe, and Zapotec—has influenced the genetic makeup of present-day Maya populations,16 the timing of its arrival remains elusive. Recent analysis of the Terminal Classic genomes from Chichén Itzá in Yucatan provides evidence of genetic continuity into a contemporary population from the same region.18
Here, we report seven newly sequenced ancient Copán genomes, all originating from the Classic Maya period (Table 1). This includes one individual (CpM13) buried in a tomb characteristic of a royal vault, but outside of the Acropolis, with grave goods suggesting that he belonged to the ruling dynasty (Data S1). CpM13 also has the highest wealth designation score of “5,” an approximate measure of social standing based on the burial form, structure, and contents. Another sequenced individual (CpM12) was discovered in close proximity to this elite individual, in a position associated with sacrificial burial,14 and assigned a wealth designation of “0.” By combining these Copán genomes with a broader ancient and modern genomic dataset spanning South America, Central America, North America, and Siberia, our study aims to enhance our understanding of the genetic ancestry and demography of the Classic Copán population, shedding light on the dynamics of population movement and social networks at the southeastern edge of the Maya region.

Results

Ancient genomes from the Classic Copán

Our initial screening focused on 16 ancient skeletal remains excavated from the sites of Copán as part of two projects (Programa Integral de Conservación del Parque Arqueológico Copán, PICPAC; Proyecto Arqueológico Copán, PROARCO), both led by Seiichi Nakamura (see Data S1). Endogenous human DNA contents above 1% were achieved for seven of these individuals (Table 1), which were then further shotgun sequenced to higher coverage, ranging from 0.033× to 0.491×. Those seven individuals are associated with five different structures (PICPAC: 10J-39, 10J-45, and 10J-71; PROARCO: 9L-104 and 9L-105). Radiocarbon dating reveals that all individuals align with the Classic Maya period and are split into two temporal groups: either the Early-to-Middle (circa 250–550 CE) or the Middle-to-Late (circa 550–830 CE) Classic Maya phase.
We confirm that all newly sequenced genomes exhibit post-mortem damage patterns (Figure S1) and short lengths of DNA fragments (Figure S2), with median lengths ranging from 37 to 42 bp, and show a low level of modern human contamination (<1.09%) (Table 1). Estimates of relatedness coefficients support potential second-degree relationships between two pairs: CpM11 and CpM34, CpM11 and CpM4243 (Figure S3). However, due to the low coverage of CpM11, these kinship estimates may be unreliable and we thus excluded this individual from all downstream analysis. The haplogroups of the remaining six individuals fall within the A2 or C1 clades, both classified as Pan American mtDNA haplogroups and commonly observed in present-day populations throughout the Americas.20,21,22 Molecular sexing identifies three males, including the possible sacrificial burial (CpM12), previously considered female based on morphological characteristics.14,23 The two males, CpM13 and CpM12, belong to the Y chromosome lineage Q1b, the most prevalent haplogroup in present-day American populations,24 while the other male, CpM11, has insufficient sequence coverage for determining the Y chromosome haplogroup. To contextualize our data within the broader genomic landscape of the Americas, we combined the Classic Copán genomes with genomic data from previously published ancient individuals (total 709 ancient genomes; Figure S4; Table S1) as well as present-day populations.
Population structure of the Classic Copán
We explored the genome-wide autosomal affinities of the Classic Copán to the other ancient populations from North, Central, and South America, along with present-day populations, using multidimensional scaling (MDS) based on outgroup-f3 measurements for all pairs of ancient and present-day populations from the Americas. This analysis separates ancient populations in a manner that reflects their geographic origins and identifies distinct clusters associated with California, the Caribbean (specifically from the Archaic periods), and the Andes (Figure 1A). All populations from Central America—comprising the Classic Copán, Early Holocene, and Late Archaic genomes from Belize,16,17 Classic and Colonial Maya genomes from Mexico,18,19 pre-Hispanic genomes from Panama,25 and present-day populations—tend to group together (Figure 1A). Notably, the present-day Maya consistently show the closest genetic affinity to the Classic Copán, regardless of the dimensions selected (Figure S5). Populations from other geographic regions, such as Brazil, Argentina, and the Ceramic Caribbean, are located close to or overlap with the Central American cluster across different dimensions in the MDS analysis (Figure S5), suggesting a potential genetic link among these populations.
This shared ancestry is further supported by our ADMIXTURE analysis at K = 9 (Figures 1B, 1C, and S6–S8), where one component (red) is prevalent across several populations, including those from Central America, the Caribbean, the Andes, Brazil, and Argentina (Figure 1B). This ancestral component is consistently high in the Central American individuals, particularly maximized in the Early Holocene (9,300–7,400 years old) and Late Archaic (5,600–3,700 years old) individuals from the Maya region (Figure 1C). In contrast, later Maya populations, such as Classic Copán, Chichén Itzá, and Mexico Colonial, tend to exhibit another component (yellow) that is dominant in Aridoamerica as well as a present-day Pima population (Figure 1C). The other ancestral components mostly mirror geography (Figure S7): cyan for the Arctic and some Early Holocene individuals from Brazil, orange for the Channel Islands, purple for the other parts of California, green for the Andes, dark blue for Patagonia, and brown for the Archaic Caribbean.

Genetic affinities to the populations from the Maya region
We investigated genetic relationships between the Classic Copán genomes and populations from across Siberia and the Americas (including 188 ancient and 12 present-day populations) by computing f4 statistics with the form of f4(Mbuti, X; Y, Copan). This analysis iterates through each of these 200 populations as X, with the remaining populations designated as Y. We then ranked the populations based on the frequency with which a population exhibits a greater genetic affinity to the Classic Copán compared with each of the 199 other populations, with a Z score > 3.0 (Figure 2A; Table S2; Data S2). This analysis reveals strong genetic affinities between the Classic Copán population and other Mesoamericans. In particular, the present-day Maya emerges as the top hit, followed by the Terminal Classic Maya from Chichén Itzá in Mexico in second place.
We then explored whether these genetic affinities are reciprocally supported across a time-transect of ancient genomes in the Maya region by replacing Copan with LateArchaic, Chichen_Itza, Mexico_Colonial, or Mayan for f4 tests, respectively (Figures 2B–2E; Table S2; Data S2). These additional analyses reveal that the top populations are consistently either earlier or later Maya groups relative to the tested population, providing strong evidence of genetic connections among these populations over time. In particular, the Early Holocene populations from Belize exhibit significant genetic affinities to the Late Archaic population in the same region, as reported in a previous study.16
The close temporal connections across populations from the Maya region are further supported by our identical-by-descent (IBD) analysis, based on refinedIBD26 with imputed data27,28 (Figures S9 and S10), followed by network-based community detection.29 The imputation performance assessed in this study is consistent with findings from a previous study that incorporated a larger panel of ancient American genomes.30 All individuals from the Late Archaic and Classic Copán, as well as most individuals from Chichén Itzá and Colonial Mexico, are grouped within a single community (“1-1”) (Figures 3 and S11), which is a part of a spatially and temporally diverse community “1” (Figures S12 and S13). This clustering largely reflects geographic and cultural contexts (Figure S12), consistent with the ADMIXTURE results (Figure 1). Together, these results suggest a certain level of genetic continuity in the Mayan region from the Late Archaic period through to the present.

Genetic ancestry of ancient and present-day Maya populations
To explore patterns of gene flow across Maya area through time, we directly compared the genetic affinities of different pairs of populations from the region (i.e., LateArchaic, Copan, Chichen_Itza, Mexico_Colonial, and Mayan) to those of all other American populations (Tables 2 and S3), using f4 statistics in the form of f4(Mbuti, X; Y, Z), where Y and Z represent a pair of Maya populations, while X is any other populations in our dataset. These f4 tests demonstrate that, compared with the Late Archaic population, the later Maya populations consistently exhibit significant affinities with a pre-Hispanic population from the Sierra Gorda—a transitional region between Aridoamerica and Mesoamerica—or with highland Mexican populations from Oaxaca, encapsulating the Mixe, Zapotec, and Mixtec. In contrast, several Early Holocene individuals, from Belize as well as from Chile and Argentina, show greater affinities with the Late Archaic than with the later Maya populations.
We used the Late Archaic, as a source of local ancestry in the Maya region, to assess gene flow from the Sierra Gorda or Oaxaca in the Classic, Colonial, and present-day populations of the Maya region (Table S4). Our admixture modeling shows that the Sierra Gorda is an unlikely source of gene flow due to tail probabilities less than 5% across the Maya populations, except for the Classic Copán. In contrast, the admixture model is confidently supported for all of them when the Zapotec, a population that consistently exhibits genetic affinities to the later Maya populations (Table 2), is used as a source of highland Mexican ancestry (Figure 4).
Late Archaic ancestry is dominant in the Classic Copán, with the proportion of highland Mexican ancestry accounting only for 6.1% ± 2.6%. This pattern is consistent across temporal groups (Table S5) and among individuals within the Classic Copán (Figure S14). In contrast, the Terminal Classic population from Chichén Itzá exhibits nearly equal contributions from both ancestral sources. These proportions appear to have remained stable in the Colonial population from Campeche but shifted back in the present-day Maya, where Late Archaic ancestry accounts for 76.5% ± 9.4%. This shift may, however, reflect the influence of a small sample size (i.e., two individuals) rather than a population-wide trend, given the substantial inter-individual variation observed in the Chichén Itzá and Colonial Mexican populations (Figure S14).
With DATES,31,32 we estimated that the admixture between Late Archaic and highland Mexican ancestry in the Chichén Itzá population occurred 497 ± 159 years ago (Figure S15). Given that a mean of median calibrated dates reported for Chichén Itzá individuals18 is 892 CE, this estimate supports the emergence of highland Mexican ancestry in the Maya region during the Early-to-Middle Classic period.

Genetic footprints of population collapse in the Maya region
We leveraged IBD segment sharing to infer temporal changes in population size. The patterns of mean total IBD lengths vary across times and regions (Figure S16), likely reflecting differences in population demography. To explore the historical factors contributing to this observed variation, we employed a sliding window approach, estimating a population size at specific time points using IBD sharing among individuals within each temporal window as input for IBDNe33 (Figure 5).
The population in the Maya region appears to have experienced significant growth in effective size, reaching approximately 19,000 circa 1,221 years BP. This increase may be associated with gene flow from highland Mexican ancestry during the Classic period, as well as the advent of agriculture,34 as supported by Classic Copán individuals who show a strong reliance on maize as a primary dietary source (Figure S17). This growth clearly stands out among the six regions analyzed. For example, in the Channel Islands or Patagonia, population sizes appear consistently small through time. The recent increase in population size in the Caribbean likely reflects the genetic, as well as cultural, transition from the Archaic to Ceramic period,35 as indicated by their IBD patterns (Figure S16). However, the population growth in the Maya region appears to reverse approximately 1,200 years BP, coinciding with the onset of the collapse of Classic Maya civilization. A decline is also observable in the Andes at around the same time. Nevertheless, the population size in the Andes rebounded in later periods, possibly linked to the rise and expansion of the Inka civilization.

Discussion
Our integrated analysis of newly generated Classic Copán genomes, in comparison with published ancient and present-day American genomes, uncovers genetic and demographic changes during the Classic Maya period. The population structure analyses of MDS, ADMIXTURE, f4 statistics, and IBD (Figures 1, 2, and 3) strongly support genetic continuity in the Maya region from the Late Archaic period to the present. However, this continuity was not exclusive, as time-stamped genomes from the region reveal the emergence of genetic ancestry associated with highland Mexican populations, potentially from a region such as Oaxaca, beginning in the Early-to-Middle Classic period.
Our analysis shows that this gene flow extended to Copán (Figure 4), located at the geographic periphery of the Classic Maya states, but was diluted relative to more northerly populations, such as that at Chichén Itzá. These findings highlight the role of mobility, potentially connected to the Maya elite network, in the formation of the Copán kingdom. The strong genetic ties from the Late Archaic period through to the present support the amalgamation of local populations with incoming members of a greater Maya network as a strategy for expansion of Maya polities, as proposed by archaeological studies.3,8,9
The population demography inferred from IBD segment sharing indicates a sharp decline in the effective size of the Maya population approximately 1,200 years ago (Figure 5), coinciding with the end of the Classic period. This period also saw frequent severe droughts, dated to between the 9th and 11th century CE, which have been considered one of several factors contributing to civil conflict, societal instability, and the collapse of the Classic Maya civilization.3,36 It is important to note that, although there is spatial variation in both the timing and intensity of these droughts, as well as in the process of societal collapse,36 such variability is not accounted for in our analysis. The estimates of population size thus reflect an overall trend for a group of ancient Maya individuals, which remain sparse in both time and space, rather than representing any specific cultural or geographic contexts.
In summary, our research offers a new perspective, suggesting the enduring persistence of local ancestry in the Maya region, while also highlighting the broader mobility beyond the conventional boundaries of the Maya territory.

Limitations of the study
There are caveats to this analysis. First, our study is constrained by the small sample size, involving only seven Classic Copán individuals, the majority of whose genomes have low coverage. These are well-known limiting factors in statistical tests and admixture modeling.37 Second, it is important to note that there is a discrepancy between the wealth designation scores assigned to individuals and their archaeological contexts. The wealth designation measures are based on the criteria outlined by a previous study,38 which may not be fully applicable to burial contexts in the ancient Copán society. Indeed, an in-depth profiling of funerary contexts associated with Maya culture reveals that nearly 90% are classified as low rank (i.e., scores of “0” or “1”), with only 5% assigned to scores of “4” or “5” (i.e., high rank),39 suggesting that the majority of the populations fall within the low-rank category. Lastly, our sampling is non-random, focusing on individuals from the same residential or patio groups (Table 1). Considering the observed archaeological and isotopic variation across Copán sites,12,13,14 as well as across the other Classic Maya states, a more extensive and denser sampling of ancient genomes will be essential to trace the temporal and regional diversity of the population within the state, which will then provide a more comprehensive view on the rise and decline of complex human societies of the Classic Maya.

STAR★Methods
Experimental model and study participant details
In this study, we successfully sequenced the genomes of seven individuals from Copán. Detailed information about their archaeological contexts is provided in Data S1. All archaeological human remains included in this study were acquired under permission for the use of archaeological samples in DNA analysis from the Honduran Institute of Anthropology and History, with authorization granted by the General Manager, Dr. Rolando Canizales. The findings of this study will be shared with the authorities at the Honduran Institute of Anthropology and History. Furthermore, the results will be disseminated among local communities through the institution.
Method details
Sampling
Petrous bones from a total 16 individuals (i.e., petrous bone fragments) excavated from the sites of Copán in western Honduras were sampled and brought for processing at Kanazawa University in Japan. Detailed information on the archaeological contexts is provided in Data S1.
Radiocarbon dating
Following the protocol on bone collagen extraction used in previous studies,55,56 we sampled 0.2–0.5 g of cortical bone from human remains using a diamond cutting tool. To remove any organic contaminants from soil, the bone surfaces were cleaned with a sand blaster and an ultrasonic cleaning device. The bone powders were decalcified in 0.4 M HCl at 4°C for 40–41 hours. The decalcified bone samples were neutralised with distilled water, and then treated with 0.1 M NaOH for 20–60 minutes. After neutralisation, the samples were then gelatinized in distilled water, acidified to 0.0001 M HCl (pH 4), and heated at 90°C for 40-43 hours.
The gelatinized samples were filtered using a glass filter (GF/F, Whatman) to remove impurities, and the dissolved gelatine was then recovered by freeze drying. Approximately 500 μg of extracted collagen was analysed to determine the atomic carbon-to-nitrogen ratios (C:N), as well as the δ13C and δ15N values. This analysis was conducted using an elemental analyser (Flash 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with an isotope ratio mass spectrometer (EA-IRMS; DELTA V and ConFlo IV, Thermo Fisher Scientific).
Isotope ratios were reported in per mille (‰) units, calculated based on differences from the carbon and nitrogen reference materials (VPDB and AIR, respectively). A commercial alanine standard (SI Science, Japan), traceable to PDB and AIR, was used to correct the isotope ratios and assess measurement errors. In this study, measurement precision for carbon and nitrogen isotope ratios was maintained at 0.2‰, taken as one standard deviation.
All samples exhibited the C:N ratios ranging from 2.9 to 3.6,57,58 except for CpM12 that had a C:N ratio of 4.1 and was therefore excluded from radiocarbon dating. For radiocarbon dating, CO2 gas derived from the gelatine was purified and reduced to graphite using hydrogen over iron powder in a vacuum line, following established protocols.59 The 14C content of the synthesised graphite was measured via accelerator mass spectrometry (AMS) at the University Museum, The University of Tokyo. Corrections to the 14C counts were applied using international standards. Additionally, the 14C content was corrected based on δ13C values measured by AMS. Conventional 14C ages (BP) were calibrated to calendar years (calBP) using OxCal 4.4 with the IntCal 20 dataset.60,61,62
DNA extraction
Bone sample processing was carried out in a dedicated ancient DNA facility at Kanazawa University where all the precautions for ancient DNA processing were followed as described in a previous study.63 Bone samples were photographed extensively before further processing. All bones were exposed to ultraviolet light for 15 min on either side to remove surface contaminants. Further cleaning of their surface was performed with a drill before extraction. The otic capsule region of the petrous temporal bone was targeted for sampling. An aliquot of 40-100 mg of the bone powder was subjected to a silica column method with an initial washing step by predigestion solution.64 DNA extracts were purified with MinElute silica columns (QIAGEN) and eluted at a volume of 30 μl of the elution buffer.
Library preparation and sequencing
The initial screening of each individual and blank controls was performed by constructing a double-stranded DNA next-generation sequencing library using a NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina with bead-based size selection. Every library was screened on a MiSeq Illumina platform. The individuals with endogenous contents between 1% and 17% were selected for high-coverage sequencing and subsequently treated with uracil-DNA-glycosylase (UDG) before a second library construction.65 The concentration and quality were then assessed using the Agilent TapeStation 4200 system with the High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape. High-coverage sequencing for UDG-treated libraries from seven individuals was carried out on a NovaSeq 6000 Illumina platform (150-bp paired-end reads) at Macrogen (Republic of Korea).
Quantification and statistical analysis
Sequence data processing
We trimmed adapters using AdapterRemoval version 2.2.2,40 with the following parameters: --collapse --minadapteroverlap 1 --minlength 25 --minquality 25 --trimqualities. The trimmed reads were aligned to the hs37d5 human reference genome using BWA (Burrows-Wheeler Aligner) version 0.7.5,41 with relaxed parameters, “bwa aln -l 16500 -n 0.01 -o 2.” We used picard-tools version 2.22.1 (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) to add reads groups, remove PCR duplicates, and merge all different libraries sequenced on MiSeq and NovaSeq platforms into a single BAM per individual. The authenticity of ancient DNA was determined in non-UDG-treated libraries by looking at degradation patterns at 5′- and 3′-end, respectively, using mapDamage2.0.42 The reads were further filtered for the length longer than 34 bp and mapping quality higher than 25. Two final processing steps were carried out on the merged files: Indels were realigned using Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) version 3.7-0,43 and the quality of the two bases at the end of each read was manually reduced to a score of 2 (“softclipped”), to avoid calling genotype from these damage-prone regions. Genomic coverage and depth were measured using qualimap version 2.2.1.44 We used the same processing pipeline for published ancient genomes in FASTQ format with the exception of using cutadapt version 1.1545 instead of AdapterRemoval. BAM files were realigned to our reference genome and then put through the same pipeline. Quality assessment of raw data was conducted with FastQC v0.11.4.46
Relatedness and molecular sex determination
The relatedness coefficient was estimated for ten bone samples that were sequenced to high coverage using “Relationship Estimation from Ancient DNA” (READ).47 This analysis identified three pairs of the bone samples as genetically identical (CpM02-CpM03; CpM05-CpM06; CpM42-CpM43; see also Data S1 and Figure S3), which were subsequently merged into single individuals (CpM0203, CpM0506, and CpM4243). We then re-ran READ for seven Classic Copán individuals. To determine the molecular sex for each of our ancient individuals, we filtered for reads with a minimum mapping quality of 30 and followed the method outlined in a previous study.66 In summary, we calculated the ratio of Y chromosome reads to the total number of sex chromosomes (Ry) and assigned female if Ry < 0.016 or male if Ry > 0.075.
Contamination and mitochondrial DNA haplogroups
To assign mitochondrial (mtDNA) haplogroups, we extracted sequence reads uniquely mapped to mtDNA from BAM files using SAMtools version 1.748 and re-aligned the reads to the revised Cambridge reference sequence using BWA version 0.7.5.41 Consensus sequences were determined to mapping quality higher than 25, a minimum depth of consensus of 5, and a base quality of 30 using mpileup and bcftools within the SAMtools package. We then assigned a specific haplotype to each consensus sequence using HaploGrep version 3.2.1.49
We estimated contamination rates as previously described.67 Briefly, we calculated the rate of secondary bases that did not match the consensus sequence at haplotype-defining and private mutations defined by HaploGrep.
Y-chromosome haplogroup determination
All newly sequenced individuals determined to be male were piled up to the 2019 version of the International Society of Genetic Genealogy database using GATK version 3.7-0,43 with a mapping quality of 20 and a base quality of 30.
Genotype calling and merging ancient with present-day genomes
We created a large panel that includes newly sequenced Classic Copán genomes and publicly available ancient genomes from Siberia and the Americas (see Table S1 and Figure S4). We genotyped all ancient individuals for the biallelic SNP sites present on two different reference panels, to which they were subsequently merged: (i) the Human Origin Array (HOA) consisting of 594,896 SNP sites for 1,963 modern, ancient, and reference genomes68 and (ii) the Simon Genome Diversity Project (SGDP) consisting of 278 modern, ancient, and reference genomes69 that have been filtered for autosomal transversion-only SNPs with a minor allele frequency of 1%, which left 3,867,550 SNP sites in our merged data. For each SNP site, we randomly called a high-quality single base (bq30) per position to create a pseudo-haploid genotype using GATK version 3.7-0.43
All present-day American individuals in both the HOA and SGDP datasets were masked for genomic regions that were identified as inherited from non-American populations, specifically Africa and West Eurasia. The worldwide populations from the HOA and SGDP datasets were classified into following groups based on geographic origins: Africa, America, East Asia, Middle East, Oceania, Siberia, South Asia and West Eurasia. We then applied MOSAIC,50 with the following parameters “—ancestries 3—panels Africa America Siberia EastAsia SouthAsia WestEurasia.” Genomic coordinates of non-American segments were obtained with the R function “grid_to_pos()” included in the Mosaic package, converting grid points based on recombination distances into physical positions. Once non-American haplotypes were identified (i.e., segments that were inherited from populations either from Africa or West Eurasia), the corresponding genomic regions were fully masked.
Multidimensional scaling (MDS)
MDS was conducted based on outgroup-f3 statistics measured for all combinations of ancient and present-day American populations from the SGDP panel, with Mbuti as the outgroup, using AdmixTools v6 package.51 Populations were excluded from MDS analysis if fewer than 100 SNPs were included in any of the tests performed for a particular population. Additionally, to improve the resolution of population structure in the Americas, specific ancient individuals from Siberia, the Arctic, AG2, MA1, Late Dorset, and Middle Dorset were excluded from the analysis.
ADMIXTURE
Unsupervised genetic clustering was conducted by ADMIXTURE version 1.2352 for ancient and present-day American individuals with at least 10,000 SNPs in the HOA panel. SNPs were pruned for sites in linkage disequilibrium using PLINK v1.90b4.4,53 with a sliding window size of 50 variants, a step size of 5 variants, and an r2 threshold of 0.2 (--indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2), leaving 184,489 SNPs for analysis. We ran 10 replicates with random seeds for the number of clusters (K) from 2 to 13. The run with the highest likelihood across the replicates was plotted. Results from K = 9 were plotted on a geographical map using mapmixture implemented in R.70
F4-statistics
We used qpDstat (version 900) for the merged datasets of ancient individuals and the SGDP panel of present-day populations, with the f4 mode in the AdmixTools v6 package.51 Mbuti was always specified as an outgroup, and standard errors in f4-statistics were calculated from the default block jackknife approach. Statistical significance in genetic affinity between populations was defined as Z-score greater than 3.0, with more than 1,000 SNPs used for the test.
Admixture modelling by qpAdm
To model admixture events in the Classic Copán and other ancient and present-day populations from the Americas, we applied qpAdm v1000 in the AdmixTools v6 package51,71 to the merged data of ancient and SGDP populations. Our analysis only used transversion sites with global minor allele frequencies of >1%, coupled with the option of “allsnps: YES.” A set of 13 ancient and present-day populations was used as an outgroup in the modelling: Han (n = 3), Pima (n = 2), Mixe (n = 3), Karitiana (n = 3),69 MA1 (n = 1),72 USR1 (n = 1),73 ASO_4000 (n = 1),74 Sumidouro5 (n = 1), Ayayema (n = 1), PuntaSantaAna (n = 1), SpiritCave (n = 1),75 Anzick (n = 1),76 and Taino (n = 1).77 We considered that an admixture model fits data if a tail probability is greater than 0.05.
Dating admixture
We used DATES v75331,32 to estimate the time of admixture between Late Archaic and Mesoamerican ancestry in the Chichén Itzá. Zapotec was used to represent highland Mexican ancestry, while the Late Archaic population was used as a source of local ancestry. The estimated date in generation was converted into years, assuming a generation time of 25 years, which was further added into a mean age of mediate dates from Chichén Itzá individuals (i.e., 1,058 years before present). The parameter settings that we used are as follows: binsize: 0.001, maxdis: 1.0, runmode: 1, mincount: 1, and lovalfit: 0.45. The SE was estimated from a weighted block jackknife method.
Imputation pipeline
We used the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 as the reference panel for imputation,78 consisting of 75,880,357 SNP sites. All bi-allelic variants were called from ancient data, and the corresponding genotype likelihoods were calculated using bcftools v1.1154 with the arguments “-mpileup” and “-call” respectively, and subsequently indexed with “-index”. The genotype likelihood score for all transition sites was manually lowered to “0” before imputation in order to account for potential errors caused by damaged ancient DNA that would influence the imputation output.
Ancient genomes were individually imputed using GLIMPSE v1.0.1.28 Most of the genomes, which were shotgun-sequenced at a genome-wide coverage below 0.05, were pre-filtered due to the relatively low imputation accuracy.79 Input genomes were first split into chunks of 2 Mb windows with a buffer size of 200 kb using GLIMPSE_chunk and subsequently imputed with GLIMPSE_phase. These chunks were then ligated using GLIMPSE_ligate. Output SNPs were further filtered based on their output genotype likelihood score, with only SNPs reaching 99% considered in further analysis.
A 6% missing rate threshold was applied to filter the GLIMPSE-imputed genomes, which resulted in 324 genomes. All autosomal SNPs present on the 1240K capture panel—a widely used SNP set for ancient human DNA—were extracted from the GLIMPSE-imputed genomes. These genomes were then subjected to a second round of imputation using BEAGLE v5.427 based on the reference panel of the 1000 Genome Phase 3. This batch imputation leveraged markers from other ancient individuals to improve the overall imputation quality. We then re-extracted the 1240K sites from BEAGLE-imputed genomes for IBD analysis.
Validation of imputation
We assessed the performance of imputation by downsampling the processed BAM file for two selected high coverage ancient individuals to lower coverages (i.e., 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5×), using DownsampleSam, part of Picard tools version 1.101 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360036431292-DownsampleSam-Picard) with random seed “1234.” We independently imputed each downsampled genomes under the same pipeline, and compared the output sites to the original sequence data. The two high coverage individuals selected for comparison were: USR1, an 11,500-year-old ancient Beringian individual from Alaska,73 and Sumidouro5, a 10,400-year-old ancient Brazilian individual.75
Diploid variants were called from processed BAM files using HaplotypeCaller, which is part of GATK version 3.7-0.43 Direct comparisons were made between sites common to the imputed and downsampled genomes. Performance was measured under two metrics, as detailed in a previous study79: a) concordance, which was defined as rate at which diploid sequence data was correctly re-created through imputation (i.e., overlapping sites divided by the sites present in the diploid data), and b) accuracy, which was defined as how often imputed data accurately matched the sequence data (i.e., overlapping sites divided by the number of imputed sites).
IBD analysis
Identical-by-descent (IBD) segments across the double-imputed genomes of 324 ancient individuals were identified using refinedIBD26 with default parameters including a LOD score of 3 and a minimum length of 1.5 cM. To correct for genotyping and haplotype-phase switching errors, which could split true IBD segments into shorter, adjacent segments, we used the merge-ibd-segments.17Jan20.102.jar utility to merge breaks or short gaps between IBD segments, removing gaps that contained at most one discordant heterozygote and were less than 0.6 cM in length.
The refinedIBD output was further used to estimate population sizes using IBDNe33 with default parameters. Individuals associated with specific geographic regions were sorted chronologically, from the oldest to the most recent. A sliding window approach was then applied for population size estimation, with each window containing five individuals and a step size of two individuals. For each window, population size was calculated as the harmonic mean of estimates between 5 and 50 generations. The corresponding time point for each population size was represented by the mean of the dates associated with the five individuals in that window. To capture temporal shifts in population size, a smoothing curve was fitted using the smooth.spline function in R, with a lambda value of 0.00005.
IBD-based network clustering by Leiden algorithm
We created a matrix representing the total lengths of IBD sharing between all pairs of 324 ancient individuals. To detect genetic clusters at a finer scale, we applied a 10 cM threshold to the matrix, setting any values below this length to zero. An undirected network was then constructed using the igraph package in R,80,81 with total IBD sharing as edge weights. We performed iterative community detection on this network using the Leiden algorithm,29 implemented in the leidenAlg package in R (v1.1.3; https://github.com/kharchenkolab/leidenAlg), with a two-step recursive clustering process and a minimum community size of 10.
