# High-coverage Jomon genomes reveal the population history of the Japanese archipelago

## Abstract

We report six newly sequenced high-coverage Jomon genomes and analyze them alongside two previously published genomes to comprehensively investigate the population history of the Japanese archipelago during the Jomon period. Principal component analysis and ADMIXTURE results demonstrate that Jomon populations were genetically highly homogeneous with no evidence of large-scale external gene flow throughout the period. However, detailed analysis using f3 and f4 statistics revealed subtle but significant genetic changes between Early Jomon Ikawazu individuals and later Jomon populations. These changes appear to reflect temporal rather than geographical differentiation, likely associated with lifestyle adaptations during the Jomon period. Polygenic risk score (PRS) analysis revealed that Jomon individuals had genetic predispositions for shorter stature and higher BMI compared to modern Japanese, along with metabolic characteristics adapted to hunter-gatherer lifestyles. This study demonstrates that high-precision genomic data can detect subtle genetic structures within populations that were previously undetectable, providing new insights into the population history of the Japanese archipelago.

## Introduction

Understanding the population history of the Japanese archipelago is crucial for elucidating the formation process of modern Japanese populations. The Jomon people, hunter-gatherers who inhabited the Japanese archipelago from approximately 16,000 to 3,000 years ago, represent one of the genetic ancestors of modern Japanese populations. Previous ancient DNA studies have established that Jomon people represent a distinct East Eurasian lineage that diverged early from other populations. However, most previous studies relied on low-coverage genomic data, limiting our understanding of genetic diversity and temporal changes within Jomon populations.

## Methods

### Samples

We analyzed eight high-coverage Jomon genomes comprising six newly reported individuals and two previously published genomes:

**Newly reported individuals:**
- Todoroki 5 (T5): Early Jomon period, Kumamoto
- Ikawazu 4,15,18 (I4,I15,I18): Middle Jomon period, Gunma  
- Mitsusawa 3 (M3): Middle Jomon period, Kanagawa
- Chidorigakubo 3 (C3): Middle Jomon period, Kanagawa

**Previously published individuals:**
- Funadomari 23 (F23): Late Jomon period, Hokkaido
- Ikawazu 1 (I1): Early Jomon period, Gunma

### Comparative datasets

1. 1240K Allen Ancient DNA Resource (primarily East Asian populations)
2. 1000 Genomes Project (Japanese population, JPT)
3. Modern Japanese allele frequency data by 47 prefectures
4. Japanese GWAS data (http://jenger.riken.jp/result)

### Population genetic analyses

- **Principal Component Analysis**: smartpca (EIGENSOFT package) version 18140
- **ADMIXTURE analysis**: ADMIXTURE 1.3.0 (K=2-10)
- **f-statistics**: qp3Pop, qpDstat, qpF4ratio (AdmixTools)
- **Phylogenetic analysis**: TreeMix version 1.13
- **Polygenic Risk Score analysis**: P-value thresholds of 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001

## Results

### Genetic structure of Jomon populations

Principal component analysis (Figure 1) revealed that high-coverage Jomon genomes formed a tight cluster overlapping with 1240K Jomon samples but with reduced scatter, indicating more accurate genetic positioning. Jomon populations were clearly separated from modern East Asian populations along PC1, positioned at the extreme negative end, confirming their unique genetic ancestry.

ADMIXTURE analysis (K=3) showed that Jomon populations were recognized as a single genetic component with no evidence of admixture with other East Asian populations (Figure 2a-e). This supports the hypothesis that there was no large-scale external gene flow during the Jomon period.

### Subtle genetic changes within Jomon populations

Despite overall genetic homogeneity, f3 statistics revealed subtle but significant genetic changes within Jomon populations (Figure 3a). Early Jomon Ikawazu individuals showed significant differences from other Jomon individuals (P < 0.05), particularly from Middle Jomon individuals (Mitsusawa 3, Chidorigakubo 3).

Prefecture-level f3 statistics analysis with modern Japanese populations (Figure 3b-f) revealed that temporal patterns were more pronounced than geographical patterns. Early Jomon Ikawazu 1 showed distinct patterns compared to other Jomon individuals, while Middle Jomon individuals clustered together temporally.

### Genetic relationships with modern populations

f4 statistics analysis (Figure 4a) confirmed significant positive values for f4(Dai, Mbuti; Japanese, Jomon) across all Jomon individuals, with Ikawazu 1 showing slightly lower genetic contribution to modern Japanese.

f4-ratio tests (Figure 4b) estimated Jomon ancestry proportions in modern Japanese:
- Comparison with Koreans: 11.5-18.6%
- Comparison with Han Chinese: 15.8-25.3%

Ikawazu-derived ancestry proportions were significantly lower than other Jomon individuals (P < 0.05).

### Phylogenetic analysis

TreeMix analysis (Figure 6) revealed that Jomon populations formed a single clade with temporal branching patterns. No migration edges were detected, supporting their isolation.

### Polygenic risk score analysis

PRS analysis revealed distinctive phenotypic characteristics of Jomon populations (Figure 7a-c):

**Body composition:**
- Height: Significantly lower scores than modern Japanese
- BMI: Higher scores than modern Japanese
- Consistent with energy-efficient body types

**Metabolic traits:**
- Lipid metabolism: Higher scores for TC, TG, LDL-C; lower HDL-C
- Glucose metabolism: Higher scores for blood glucose, HbA1c
- Pattern consistent with "thrifty genotype" hypothesis

**Liver function:**
- Liver enzymes (GGT, AST, ALT): Higher scores (enhanced detoxification capacity)
- Synthesis markers (albumin, A/G ratio): Lower scores

**Kidney function:**
- eGFR: Higher scores (good kidney function)
- Electrolyte balance: Patterns suggesting high-protein diet adaptation

**Cardiovascular system:**
- Cardiac function indicators (EF, FS): Lower scores
- Low modern cardiovascular disease risk but adapted to intense physical activity

**Immune-related traits:**
- Neutrophils and WBC: Higher scores (immediate bacterial infection response)
- Eosinophils/basophils: Lower scores (low parasitic infection risk environment)

## Discussion

### Genetic homogeneity and isolation

High-coverage genomic data clearly demonstrated that Jomon populations were genetically highly homogeneous. ADMIXTURE and TreeMix analyses support the absence of large-scale external gene flow throughout the Jomon period. This prolonged genetic isolation likely resulted from the geographical isolation of the Japanese archipelago and maintenance of distinct cultural identity.

### Temporal changes within Jomon populations

f3 and f4 statistics detected subtle but significant genetic changes between Early Jomon (Ikawazu) and later Jomon populations. This temporal differentiation may reflect:

1. **Environmental adaptation**: Climate warming (Jomon transgression) from Early to Middle Jomon periods
2. **Population dynamics**: Bottleneck or founder effects during population expansion
3. **Subsistence changes**: Shifts toward more stable food acquisition strategies

Importantly, these changes represent temporal rather than geographical differentiation, as demonstrated by prefecture-level f3 statistics showing temporal clustering over geographical patterns.

### Adaptation to hunter-gatherer lifestyle

PRS analysis revealed that Jomon populations were highly adapted to hunter-gatherer lifestyles:

**Metabolic adaptation:** High lipid and glucose metabolism scores support the "thrifty genotype hypothesis," reflecting adaptation to unstable food supply. These genetic characteristics may become risk factors for lifestyle diseases in modern affluent environments.

**Body composition:** The combination of short stature and high BMI suggests adaptation to energy-efficient body types, providing survival advantages under limited food resources.

**Immune characteristics:** High neutrophil and WBC scores reflect capacity for immediate response to injuries and acute infections common in hunter-gatherer lifestyles. Low eosinophil and basophil scores suggest lower parasitic infection risks compared to agricultural societies.

### Implications for modern Japanese formation

f4-ratio tests estimated Jomon ancestry contribution to modern Japanese at 11.5-25.3% depending on the comparison population. The slightly lower contribution from Early Jomon Ikawazu individuals suggests that admixture with Yayoi-period immigrant populations primarily occurred with Middle Jomon and later populations.

### Evolutionary medicine implications

The genetic characteristics of Jomon populations provide important insights for understanding modern Japanese disease susceptibility:

1. **Metabolic syndrome**: Jomon-type metabolic adaptations may increase risk for type 2 diabetes and dyslipidemia in modern high-calorie environments
2. **Cardiovascular disease**: Interactions between Jomon cardiovascular characteristics and modern risk factors
3. **Autoimmune diseases**: Evolutionary changes in immune systems and divergence from modern immune balance

### Study limitations

This study has several limitations: (1) geographical bias toward eastern Japan samples with insufficient western Japan data, (2) temporal gaps with few Late Jomon samples, and (3) lack of functional validation for PRS analyses. Future studies require broader geographical and temporal sampling and integrated analyses with other ancient DNA studies.

## Conclusions

This study comprehensively analyzed the genetic characteristics of Jomon populations using eight high-coverage genomes. Major findings include:

1. Jomon populations were genetically highly homogeneous and isolated throughout the period
2. Subtle genetic changes occurred from Early to Middle Jomon periods
3. These changes represent temporal rather than geographical differentiation
4. Jomon populations possessed genetic characteristics highly adapted to hunter-gatherer lifestyles
5. These characteristics may influence modern Japanese disease susceptibility

This study demonstrates that high-precision genomic data can reveal subtle genetic structures within populations that were previously undetectable, deepening our understanding of human population history in the Japanese archipelago.
